A day with ADE

One engineer, four steps.

Step 1 · You

Pick your persona; ADE’s roles plan, build and review.

Step 2 · Your context, tools and access

Sign in once; every change to a tool asks first.

Step 3 · Projects and customers

A focus agent per project or customer, several at once.

Step 4 · Run it from the terminal or Telegram

ade ui at your desk, your own Telegram bot on your phone.

The brain

Build your persona over time, be great at what you do.

1. Pick who it is.

One of 13 personas (staff engineer, DevOps/SRE, data engineer, security engineer, fractional CTO …) or your own. bin/init --persona devops-sre --client "a fintech client" makes a private hub: one persona, one client, with its habits, work folders and suggested model.

2. Give it the work.

bin/new projects/shop --repo ... brings in each project: its own agent, state and access list; the client’s code stays in its own repos.

3. It starts each day knowing what is live.

A morning briefing: what is open, waiting and stale. Every session reads the folders’ state and the journal, so no agent starts from zero.

4. It learns how you work, with your yes.

Correct an agent or repeat a preference and it records the rule in your words; it takes effect only after you confirm it in your own terminal. At most 30 lines load into each session; bin/learn shows, edits or forgets any of them.

5. It remembers everything, locally.

bin/brain search "what did the client decide about SMS" finds any note, decision or journal entry by keyword and meaning, on your machine; nothing leaves it.

One hub per client, so clients never mix. Over weeks, the persona knows the client the way a senior engineer does.

Scenarios

How people use ADE.

Jan, a new software engineer

  1. Hired. Jan joins a company as a software engineer.
  2. Sets up a copilot. ade hub new with the staff software engineer persona: Jan’s own hub, private, with the company’s projects added by ade project add.
  3. Uses it daily. A morning briefing, a focus agent per ticket, the tests as the judge of Done. Over weeks it learns how Jan works, with Jan’s yes.
  4. Runs tasks from the phone. Away from the desk, Jan sends a task to his own Telegram bot and gets “Done” back with proof: the tests passed, and a picture of the agent’s screen.
  5. Delivers value. Tickets ship reviewed and tested, so Jan gives his company and its customers more working software, sooner.

Alvin, a new hands-on CTO

  1. Takes the job. Alvin joins a startup as its CTO and still writes code.
  2. Sets up a hub. ade hub new with the fractional CTO persona: the company’s projects, its architecture decisions and the team’s rules in one private hub.
  3. Leads and builds. Alvin writes the stories and approves them; ade dispatch sends them to the projects’ agents, and each change is reviewed by its risk before it counts.
  4. Stays on top of it. The morning briefing says what is open, waiting and stale across every project, and bin/brain search finds any past decision in seconds.

Robert, a one-person software consultancy

  1. Works alone. Robert sells software engineering and consulting to several clients, with no team behind him.
  2. One hub per client. ade hub new once per client, so their code, rules and secrets never mix.
  3. Gets a team’s process. Agents plan, build and review each client’s work; nothing counts as done until the tests pass, so Robert ships what a small team would.
  4. Runs it from anywhere. ade dispatch sends tasks to every client’s projects at once; Robert follows them on Telegram and approves at the terminal.

Sam, on call for the platform

  1. Gets paged. Something is wrong in production at night.
  2. Asks in plain words. From the phone, on Sam’s own Telegram bot: “check why the API is slow”.
  3. Stays in control. The DevOps/SRE persona investigates and drafts the fix; nothing touches production without Sam’s yes.

Ana, Ben and Chris, three engineers sharing one brain

  1. One hub. Ana makes the team’s hub with ade hub new; Ben and Chris join it with ade hub import <url> --share.
  2. Shared memory. The journal, the decisions and each project’s state are the same for all three; bin/sync keeps them current, so every session knows what the others did.
  3. Own agents, own keys. Each runs their own focus agents on their own tickets and sets their own keys; no key is shared.
  4. One bar for Done. The same rules and the same tests for everyone, so nobody’s work counts until the tests pass.

Mia teaching Leo, a senior and a junior engineer

  1. Shares the hub. Mia brings Leo into the team’s hub with ade hub import <url> --share.
  2. Learns from the record. Leo’s agents read the same journal, decisions and project notes Mia’s do; bin/brain search finds why something was done.
  3. Builds with guard rails. Leo takes his own tickets with his own agents; the tests decide Done, and each change is reviewed by its risk before it counts.
  4. Reviews what matters. Mia reads Leo’s stories and reviews, not every line, and spends her time on the decisions he can’t make yet.

In every scenario, the human runs the connection: the people, the decisions and the yes. AI agents run the execution and the support.

How it works

Ticket to Done.

  1. Ticket

    A small story with a risk level.

  2. Build

    A failing test first, then the code.

  3. Review

    Scaled to the risk.

  4. Verified

    The reviewer, never the builder, runs the tests.

  5. Done

    Green only. Red goes back to the builder.

Secure storage

Keys stay in secure storage.

A key is typed once, at a hidden prompt in your own terminal. Agents never see the value.

  • Where it lives: the macOS Keychain. On Linux, pass (encrypted), or private owner-only files, which bin/doctor calls the weaker store.
  • How it goes in: bin/keys set, at a hidden prompt. Never a chat, a file or a command line.
  • How it is used: Claude Code asks for it through its key helper when it needs it. The hub's files hold names only.
  • How it is shared: bin/keys share seals it to one person; bin/keys receive opens it into their own storage.
